\chapter{Mathematical modelling of orographic flows}
\label{chap:MODELLING}

In the previous chapter \Cref{chap:FLOWS}, the underlying physical laws governing atmospheric flows have been described. In this chapter we describe further \emph{modelling choices} used to obtain numerical solution to our problem.

\section{Initial and boundary conditions}
\label{sec:IBCs}

Let $T>0$ and denote $\Omega \subset \R^2$ to be the set

\begin{equations}[single,!,numberline=all]
\label{eq:OMEGA}
\Omega = \left\{\left(x, y\right) \in \R^2 \middle| x \in \left(-L, L\right), h(x) < y < H \right\},
\end{equations}

where $L > 0$ is the \textcolor{gray}{half-}width of the domain, $H$ is the height of the domain and $h(x)$ is the \emph{mountain profile}. In our simulations, the \emph{Witch of Agnesi} mountain profile \Cref{fig:WITCH-OF-AGNESI} of height $h_m$ and half-width $a$ has been used.

Denote further

\begin{equations}[columns,!,numberline=all]
\label{eq:BOUNDARY}
\partial \Omega &= \Gamma_L \cup \Gamma_R \cup \Gamma_B \cup \Gamma_T, \\
\Gamma_L &= \left\{-L\right\} \times \left(h(-L), H\right), \\
\Gamma_R &= \left\{L\right\} \times \left(h(L), H\right), \\
\Gamma_B &= \left(-L, L\right) \times \left\{h(x) \right\}, \\
\Gamma_T &= \left(-L, L\right) \times \left\{H \right\}, 
\end{equations}

to be the left, right, bottom, and top boundaries. 

We are interested in numerical solutions to the initial-boundary value problem for the Euler system written in the form\sidenote{This form is common in mathematical literature, as the order of the equation and the convective \emph{nonlinearity} is clearly visible.}

\begin{equations}[columns,!,numberline=all]
\label{eq:EULER_BC}
\partial_t\left(\rho \vb{v}\right) + \div\left(\rho \vb{v} \otimes \vb{v}\right) &= - \grad p + \rho \vb{g}, \, \text{in} \, \left(0,T\right) \times \Omega, \\
\partial_t \rho + \div\left(\rho \vb{v}\right)&= 0, \, \text{in} \, \left(0,T\right) \times \Omega, \\
\partial_t\left(\rho \theta\right) + \div\left(\rho \theta \vb{v}\right) &= 0, \, \text{in} \, \left(0,T\right) \times \Omega, \\
p &= R \rho T, \\
\vb{v} &= \vb{v}_0, \, \text{on} \, \left\{ 0 \right\} \times \Omega,\\
\rho &= \bar{\rho}(y), \, \text{on} \, \left\{ 0 \right\} \times \Omega,\\
\theta &= \bar{\theta}(y), \, \text{on} \, \left\{ 0 \right\} \times \Omega,\\
\vb{v} &= u_0 \vb{e}_x, \, \text{on} \, \left(0,T\right) \times \Gamma_L,\\
\theta = \bar{\theta}(y), \, \text{on} \, \left(0,T\right) \times \Gamma_L, \\
p &= \bar{p}, \, \text{on} \, \left(0,T\right) \times \Gamma_R,\\
\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} &= 0, \, \text{on} \, \left(0,T\right) \times \Gamma_B,\\
&\text{\enquote{radiation condition, on $\left(0,T\right) \times \Gamma_T$}}
\end{equations}

with $\vb{v}_0, \rho_0$ being the initial velocity and density, $\bar{p}, \bar{\theta}$ the hydrostatic profile and $0 < u_0 < c$ the constant \emph{subsonic} inflow velocity magnitude. We take $\vb{v}_0 = u_0 \vb{e_x}$, \ie we assume the flow is developed at the initialization.
The boundary conditions deserve comments:
\begin{compactitem}
	\item the inflow velocity field is constant and subsonic
	\item the bottom boundary condition is the \emph{no-penetration condition}
	\item the outflow boundary condition is \enquote{free outflow}: the fluid exits the domain with hydrostatic pressure
\item the top boundary condition is artificial: the radiation condition is prescribed \enquote{at infinity}, and hence is not easily specified \emph{at the continuous level}.
\end{compactitem}

In the forthcoming sections, we briefly discuss the specifics of our boundary conditions in more detail.

\subsection{Boundary conditions for the Euler system}
\label{sec:EULER_BC}

It is well known that the Euler system is a \emph{hyperbolic system of conservation laws} \autocite{oligerTheoreticalPracticalAspects1978,evansPartialDifferentialEquations2022}. The topic of suitable boundary conditions\sidenote{\enquote{Suitable boundary conditions} here are boundary conditions that lead to a well-posed problem.} for these systems is more involved than in the case of parabolic (or elliptic) equations.

The Euler system \textcolor{gray}{in $\R^d$} can be written in the form

\begin{equations}[columns,*]
\partial_t \rho + \div\left(\rho \vb{v}\right) &= 0, \\
\partial_t\left(\rho \vb{v}\right) + \div\left(\rho \vb{v} \otimes \vb{v} + p \identityM\right) &= \vb{0}, \\
\partial_t\left(\rho E\right) + \div\left(\left(\rho E + p\right) \vb{v}\right) &= 0,
\end{equations}

with $E = \frac{1}{2}\abs{\vb{v}}^{2} + e, e = c_V T$. Upon denoting 

\begin{equations}[single,!,numberline=all]
\label{eq:conservative_variables}
\vb{u} = \begin{bmatrix}
	\rho \\
\rho \vb{v} \\
\rho E
\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{d+2}, 
\tensorq{F} = \begin{bmatrix}
\left(\rho \vb{v}\right)^{\transpose}\\
\rho \vb{v} \otimes \vb{v} + p \identityM\\
\left(\left(\rho E + p\right)\vb{v}\right)^{\transpose}
\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{d+2 \times d},
\end{equations}

the Euler system reads simply

\begin{equations}[single,!,numberline=all]
\label{eq:EULER_CONSERVATIVE}
\partial_t \vb{u} + \div_x \tensorq{F}\left(\vb{u}\right) = \vb{0}.
\end{equations}

The vector $\vb{u}$ is called the \emph{state vector} and the vector $\div_x \tensorq{F}\left(\vb{u}\right) = \sum_{m=1}^{d}\pdv{\vb{f}_m}{x_m}\left(\vb{u}\right), \tensorq{F} = \Big[\vb{f}_1\left(\vb{u}\right) \Big| \dots \Big| \vb{f}_{d}\left(\vb{u}\right)\Big]$ is the \emph{flux vector}. Notice \Cref{eq:EULER_CONSERVATIVE} is a \emph{1st order} system. Assume now \emph{further} that $\vb{u} \mapsto \tensorq{F}\left(\vb{u}\right)$ is continuously differentiable, than the chain rule yields

\begin{equations}[single,*]
\div_x \tensorq{F}\left(\vb{u}\right) = \sum_{m=1}^{d} \pdv{\vb{f}_m}{x_m}\left(\vb{u}\right) = \sum_{m=1}^{d} \grad_{\vb{u}} \vb{f}_m  \pdv{\vb{u}}{x_m},
\end{equations}

and so we see that under the differentiability assumption, the Euler system can be recast to the form of a \emph{1st order quasilinear system}

\begin{equations}[single,*]
\partial_t \vb{u} + \sum_{m=1}^d \grad_{\vb{u}}\vb{f}_m \pdv{\vb{u}}{x_m} = 0.
\end{equations}

For $\vb{n} \in \R^d, \abs{\vb{n}} = 1$ the quantity $\vb{P}\left(\vb{u}, \vb{n}\right) = \vb{F}\left(\vb{u}\right) \vb{n} = \sum_{m=1}^d \vb{f}_m\left(\vb{u}\right)n_m$ is the \emph{physical flux} of $\vb{u}$ in the direction $\vb{n}$. Of central importance in the analysis of hyperbolic conservation laws is the Jacobi matrix of $\vb{P}$, \ie the function

\begin{equations}[single,*]
\tensorq{P}\left(\vb{u}, \vb{n}\right) = \grad_{\vb{u}}\vb{P}\left(\vb{u}, \vb{n}\right) = \sum_{m=1}^d \grad_{\vb{u}} \vb{f}_m n_m \in \R^{d+2 \times d+2}.
\end{equations}

It can be shown \autocite[p.~578]{evansPartialDifferentialEquations2022} that the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{d+2}$ of $\tensorq{P}\left(\vb{u}, \vb{n}\right)$ are real and have the form
\begin{equations}[single,*]
\lambda_1 = \vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} - c, \quad \lambda_2 = \dots = \lambda_{d+1} = \vb{v} \cdot \vb{n}, \quad \lambda_{d+2} = \vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} + c \eqcomma
\end{equations}
with $c$ the \textcolor{gray}{local} speed of sound. 

Let $\vb{n}$ denote the \emph{outward} unit normal at a boundary point. The sign of an eigenvalue then gives the orientation of the associated \emph{characteristic} relative to $\Omega$ \autocite[p.~7]{oligerTheoreticalPracticalAspects1978}:
\begin{compactitem}
	\item a \emph{negative} eigenvalue corresponds to a characteristic entering $\Omega$, so a boundary condition \emph{must} be prescribed there, otherwise the problem is underdetermined;
	\item a \emph{positive} eigenvalue corresponds to a characteristic leaving $\Omega$, so a boundary condition \emph{cannot} be prescribed there, otherwise the problem is overdetermined.
\end{compactitem}

In a system of conservation laws, the state vector $\vb{u}$ is conserved - once it is specified how $\vb{u}$ enters $\Omega$, the system is determined and \enquote{no information is lost}. Subsequently, on $\partial \Omega$ we only can specify how the state vectors $\vb{u}$ flows into $\Omega$ - and its evolution inside $\Omega$ is then fully described by the equation itself.

A subsonic inflow is characterised by $\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} < 0$ and $\abs{\vb{v}} < c$, whence
\begin{equations}[single,*]
\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} - c < 0, \quad \vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} < 0, \quad \vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} + c \geq c - \abs{\vb{v}} > 0 \eqcomma
\end{equations}
where the eigenvalue $\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n}$ has multiplicity $d$. There are therefore $d + 1$ negative eigenvalues, so $d + 1$ \textcolor{gray}{scalar} boundary conditions must be prescribed at a subsonic inflow, that is \emph{three} in two dimensions. A subsonic outflow is characterised by $\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} > 0$ and $\abs{\vb{v}} < c$, whence
\begin{equations}[single,*]
\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} - c \leq \abs{\vb{v}} - c < 0, \quad \vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} > 0, \quad \vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} + c > 0 \eqcomma
\end{equations}
where now the multiple eigenvalue $\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n}$ is positive, leaving $\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} - c$ as the only negative eigenvalue, so a single \textcolor{gray}{scalar} boundary condition must be prescribed.

Since in our case the inflow is deeply subsonic (the air enters $\Omega$ at about $\sim \SI{20}{m/s}$, see \Cref{chap:EXPERIMENTS} for details) and inside $\Omega$ the air only flows around an obstacle (without any additional interaction mechanism), we assume also that the outflow is subsonic. Consequently, we prescribe three scalar conditions at the inflow $\Gamma_L$, the two components of $\vb{v} = u_0 \vb{e}_x$ together with the thermodynamic condition $\theta = \bar{\theta}(y)$, and one scalar condition at the outflow $\Gamma_R$, the pressure $p = \bar{p}$. The boundary conditions \Cref{eq:EULER_BC} are thus sensible.

Let us conclude with some remarks and further references. The choice of suitable boundary conditions for the Euler system, and for nonlinear systems of hyperbolic conservation laws in general, remains an active field of research in both the analysis of partial differential equations and in numerical analysis. On the analytical side, well-posedness of the initial-boundary value problem for scalar conservation laws has been pioneered by \autocite{bardosFirstOrderQuasilinear1979}, and the theory for systems continues to develop \autocite{spinoloInformalAccountRecent2024}. On the numerical side, the construction of various boundary conditions (non-reflecting, far-field, open) has a long history \autocite{hedstromNonreflectingBoundaryConditions1979,roeRemoteBoundaryConditions1989}, and the design of provably well-posed boundary treatments is still actively pursued \autocite{nordstromOpenBoundaryConditions2025}.

\subsection{Radiation boundary condition}
\label{sec:RADIATION}

We are left to resolve the boundary condition at the top part of the domain. Since we are interested in internal gravity waves, the most natural BC would be the \emph{radiation boundary condition}, as described in \Cref{sec:FLOW-CORRUGATION}: impose that no energy arrives from infinity and consequently there are no reflections at the \enquote{top boundary}. The radiation boundary condition is realistic on \emph{unbounded} domains. However, in our simulations, we are dealing with bounded domains - so the radiation boundary condition \emph{cannot} be imposed on $\Gamma_{T}$. 
To be able to numerically simulate the problem, one has to work with bounded domains. Note that in this sense $\Gamma_T$ is in fact an artificial boundary, introduced only to tackle the numerical computation. The question is whether it is possible to prescribe an \emph{artificial} boundary condition on $\Gamma_T$ that would simulate the radiation boundary condition on an unbounded domain. 
Unfortunately, it can be shown \autocite{gustafssonBoundaryConditionsTime1979,baylissRadiationBoundaryConditions1980} that in a general case without any a priori knowledge of the solution's behaviour at infinity, nonreflecting boundary conditions \emph{cannot be constructed} on $\Gamma_T$. Hence in practical cases, the radiation (and nonreflecting) BCs have to be approximated.

For the wave-equation (under some symmetry assumptions), the solution can be asymptotically expanded into an infinite sum of certain functions of the spherical coordinates $\left(r, \theta, \varphi\right)$. An approach studied in \autocite{baylissRadiationBoundaryConditions1980} consists of constructing operators that annihilate the leading terms in the solution's expansion as $r \to \infty$.

Klemp and Durran in \autocite{klempUpperBoundaryCondition1983} have devised a top boundary condition permitting internal gravity waves radiation without any reflection based on information provided by the Fourier transform of the velocity field at the top boundary. In \autocite{chiniAnalysisKlempDurran2003}\autocite[p.~429]{durranNumericalMethodsFluid2010} it is shown that this boundary condition is efficient.

Nonetheless, in our simulations, a simpler boundary condition inspired by the experiments in \autocite{klempNumericalSimulationHydrostatic1978} has been used. The implementation lies in introducing an artificial damping layer near the top boundary, in which the vertically propagating internal gravity waves are damped. Klemp and Lilly in \autocite[p.~80]{klempNumericalSimulationHydrostatic1978} used a friction damping profile $\nu\left(z\right)$ with the structure

\begin{equations}[single,!,numberline=all]
\label{eq:DAMPING}
\nu(z) = \nu_t \sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2} \frac{z - z_b}{z_t - z_b}\right),
\end{equations}

where $\nu_t$ is a friction parameter, $z_b,z_t$ are the vertical coordinates of the bottom and top boundaries of the damping layer.

The implementation of the damping layer and viscosity is described in \Cref{sec:radiation_BC}.

\subsection{Terrain-following coordinates}
\label{sec:TERRAIN-FOLLOWING}

The domain \Cref{eq:OMEGA} has a non-flat lower boundary $\Gamma_B$ given by the mountain profile $h(x)$, which generally does not coincide with a Cartesian coordinate line $y=0$. It is computationally more complicated to impose a boundary condition on a non-flat $\Gamma_B$\sidenote{Recall we are imposing a no-permeability (free slip) condition $\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n}$ on $\Gamma_B$, so we \eg need to compute $\vb{n}$ on $y = h(\vb{x})$.}, hence we make use of the \emph{terrain-following coordinate transformation}. This transformation resolves the complication of a non-flat boundary by stretching the vertical coordinate so that the ground becomes a coordinate surface, the stretching equalling the terrain at the bottom and decreasing to the identity at the top. The horizontal coordinate is left untouched. Schematically, the transformation is shown in \Cref{fig:TERRAIN-FOLLOWING}.

The original construction, introduced by Phillips in \autocite{phillipsCOORDINATESYSTEMHAVING1957}, utilizes a pressure-based transformation. We follow instead the height-based transformation of Gal-Chen and Somerville \autocite{gal-chenUseCoordinateTransformation1975},
\begin{equations}[single,!,numberline=all]
\label{eq:TERRAIN-FOLLOWING}
\bar y = H\, \frac{y - h(x)}{H - h(x)} \eqcomma
\end{equations}
which maps the \textcolor{gray}{physical} ground $y = h(x)$ to $\bar y = 0$ and the top boundary $y = H$ to $\bar y = H$, and reduces to the identity where the terrain is flat. This transformation has later been adopted by Clark \autocite{clarkSmallscaleDynamicModel1977} for mountain-wave simulations.

In a grid-based method the mesh is built in the computational coordinates $(x, \bar y)$, so the non-flat ground becomes a flat coordinate line and the condition $\vb{v} \cdot \vb{n} = 0$ on $\Gamma_B$ reduces simply to a condition on that line. The price is that the governing equations must be rewritten in the new coordinate system. Every spatial derivative obtains extra chain-rule (metric) terms and the density and fluxes need to weighted by the Jacobian of \Cref{eq:TERRAIN-FOLLOWING}; this transformed form of the equations is then solved numerically.

The situation is fundamentally different for a meshless Lagrangian method.\sidenote{See \Cref{sec:SPH_OVERVIEW} for overview of Lagrangian methods.} In the SPH method to be introduced in \Cref{chap:SPH} the particles carry the fields and follow physical trajectories in the Cartesian frame, and the kernel interpolation uses physical particle distances. There is therefore no mesh to match the terrain and \emph{no need to transform} the governing equations, which are solved in their Cartesian form throughout.

In our simulations, we use the transformation \Cref{eq:TERRAIN-FOLLOWING} \emph{only} to initialise the particle configuration: the particles are created on a terrain-following lattice whose rows lie on the mountain near the ground and flatten towards the top boundary, as seen in \Cref{fig:TERRAIN-FOLLOWING}. Once the flow evolves the fluid particles advect freely and the latice structure is \emph{not preserved}, whereas the wall particles representing $\Gamma_B$ remain on the mountain surface (their position is not updated). The initialisation is described in \Cref{chap:IMPLEMENTATION}.

\subsection{Preservation of the hydrostatic balance}
\label{sec:INEXACT}

Internal gravity waves can be considered perturbations of the background atmospheric state. It is standard to take the background atmospheric state as \emph{hydrostatic}\sidenote{As we did in the section \Cref{sec:MTN-WVS}.}: $\vb{v} = 0$ and the pressure $p$ and density $\rho$ solve the equation \Cref{eq:HYDROSTATIC-P}

\begin{equations}[single,*]
	\grad p = \rho \vb{g}.
\end{equations}

Suppose now we wish to \emph{numerically} solve the \emph{non-stationary} isentropic compressible Euler system with the initial condition given as the hydrostatic balance, \ie solve\sidenote{We are using a formulation of the Euler equations that is more common in numerical mathematics.}

\begin{equations}[columns,!,numberline=all]
	\partial_t \rho + \div\left(\rho \vb{v}\right) &= 0, \\
	\partial_t\left(\rho \vb{v}\right) + \div\left(\rho \vb{v} \otimes \vb{v}\right) &= - \grad p + \rho \vb{g},\label{eq:NONSTAT-EULER}\\
	\partial_t \theta + \div\left(\theta \vb{v}\right) &= 0, \\
	\vb{v}\left(0, \vb{x}\right) = \vb{0}, \theta\left(0, \vb{x}\right) &= \theta_0\left(\vb{x}\right), \rho\left(0, \vb{x}\right) = \rho_0\exp\left(- \frac{z}{H_s}\right),
\end{equations}

together with given $p = p\left(T, \rho\right)$ and some boundary conditions.\sidenote{A rigorous numerical treatment of the boundary conditions is complicated, see \eg \autocite[p.~59]{levequeFiniteVolumeMethods2002}.} \emph{Mathematically} speaking, since at time $t=0$ the system is in hydrostatic equilibrium, the right-hand side of the momentum equation as well as the convective term vanish and the system \emph{remains} in the hydrostatic equilibrium. Yet, in a numerical simulation, generally the hydrostatic equilibrium is \emph{not preserved},

\begin{equations}[single,*]
	\grad_h p_{i} - \rho_i \vb{g} \neq 0,
\end{equations}

where $p_i, \rho_i$ are some approximations of the solution \enquote{at time $i$} obtained by the numerical method and $\grad_{h}$ is some discrete gradient operator. Since the right-hand side of the momentum balance \Cref{eq:NONSTAT-EULER} does not vanish on the \emph{discrete level}, a spurious non-physical force is introduced and the gas moves with \emph{nonzero velocity.}

\subsection{Implications for wave simulations}
\label{sec:WB-WAVES}

The failure to preserve hydrostatic balance is particularly damaging for simulations of internal gravity waves. As established earlier, internal gravity waves are small perturbations of the \emph{background state.} When the hydrostatic balance is inexact, the spurious forces influence the simulation and affect the flow field. 

Käppeli and Mishra demonstrate empirically in \autocite[p.~6]{kappeliWellbalancedFiniteVolume2016a} that, for small perturbations of the velocity field\sidenote{The authors used a sine perturbation of the form \begin{equations}[single,*]
		\delta v = A\sin\left(\omega t\right),
	\end{equations}
with A = \num{e-8}.}
spurious forces dominate the wave field. By contrast, well-balanced schemes give qualitatively correct wave dynamics. For large-amplitude flows, in \autocite[p.~6]{kappeliWellbalancedFiniteVolume2016a} it is shown that the effect is not critical. 
For small-amplitude wave dynamics---relevant to internal gravity waves---exact discrete hydrostatic balance is therefore essential.

\subsection{Well-balanced schemes}
\label{sec:WB-SCHEMES}

Numerical methods that preserve the \emph{analytic} steady solutions on the \emph{discrete} level are called \emph{well-balanced} \autocite{cargoSchemaEquilibreAdapte1994,greenbergWellBalancedSchemeNumerical1996}. The methods had been first used for shallow water equations \autocite{greenbergWellBalancedSchemeNumerical1996} and are nowadays standard in many areas where a numerical solution of \emph{hyperbolic} PDEs is required, \eg atmospheric flows \autocite{kappeliWellbalancedFiniteVolume2016a,chertockWellbalancedNumericalMethod2023,berthonFullyWellbalancedEntropystable2025} and astrophysics \autocite{kappeliWellbalancedMethodsComputational2022}. 

While well-balanced finite-volume and DG schemes are now standard for atmospheric and astrophysical applications, well-balanced SPH formulations remain underdeveloped. This thesis aims to contribute to that gap. In \Cref{sec:WELL-BALANCED-SPH} we derive well-balanced formulations of SPH suitable for isentropic atmospheric flow --- the central contribution of this thesis.
